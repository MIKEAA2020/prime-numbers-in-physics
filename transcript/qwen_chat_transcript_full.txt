The **Past Hypothesis** is the postulate that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, and that this special initial condition is what grounds the thermodynamic arrow of time. It is widely regarded as explanatorily powerful but also deeply unsatisfying, because it appears to be a brute, fine-tuned boundary condition. A number of alternatives have been proposed, ranging from new cosmological dynamics to radical reconceptualizations of time itself.

### 🔄 Dynamical and Cosmological Alternatives

These proposals seek to derive the arrow of time from **typical** dynamics rather than a special initial state.

- **Carroll–Chen model (eternal universe with unbounded entropy).** Sean Carroll and Jennifer Chen propose that the universe is eternal and that the relevant entropy measure is **unbounded**. On this view, entropy does not have a maximum; instead, a typical history has a **U-shaped entropy profile**, with a minimum at some moment and entropy increasing in *both* time directions away from that minimum. Observers on either side of the minimum would identify the direction toward the minimum as “past.” The arrow of time therefore becomes a **typical feature** of universes, not a consequence of an atypical low-entropy beginning.

- **Barbour–Koslowski–Mercati (gravitational complexity).** Julian Barbour and collaborators offer a **relationalist** alternative in which the arrow is not primarily entropic but is tied to a notion of **shape complexity** in a Newtonian gravitating universe. The “Janus point” is the moment of minimal complexity, and the arrow emerges from the growth of complexity in both directions.

- **Weak gravity / phase transition.** Some proposals suggest that the early universe was not low-entropy in an absolute sense; rather, **gravity was weaker** at early times, altering which configurations count as low-entropy. A phase transition that strengthened gravity later could then make the early state appear low-entropy relative to the present.

- **Regime condensation cosmology.** This approach attempts to generate low-entropy initial states as a **structural consequence** of condensation dynamics, without invoking special initial conditions, cycles, or singular boundary states.

- **State-to-state / steady-state cosmology.** Older steady-state models treat all times as statistically equivalent, specifying the universe at any epoch rather than privileging a special past.

### ⚛️ Quantum and Gravitational Alternatives

These approaches locate the origin of the arrow in quantum gravity, quantum cosmology, or the nature of quantum states.

- **Penrose’s Weyl Curvature Hypothesis (WCH).** Roger Penrose argues that the low entropy of the early universe is really a statement about **gravitational entropy**: the initial singularity had **vanishing Weyl curvature**, meaning a highly uniform gravitational field. The WCH is a candidate **quantum-gravity boundary condition** that would explain the low-entropy past as a consequence of deeper physics, rather than as a brute statistical postulate.

- **Bunch–Davies vacuum as the Past Hypothesis.** David Wallace and others have argued that current cosmology points to a unique pure quantum state—the **local quantum vacuum** (Bunch–Davies vacuum)—as the initial state of the universe. On this view, **statistical-mechanical probabilities emerge from quantum probabilities** without any need for an additional statistical postulate. The Past Hypothesis is thereby replaced by a quantum-cosmological boundary condition.

- **Thermal time hypothesis (Rovelli).** Carlo Rovelli’s thermal time hypothesis proposes that the **flow of time is not fundamental** but is **induced by the statistical state** of a system. Time emerges from thermodynamics, rather than thermodynamics needing a special initial time-condition. This dissolves the need for a Past Hypothesis by making temporal flow **state-dependent** rather than a universal background feature.

### 🧠 Philosophical and Epistemic Alternatives

These positions deny that the Past Hypothesis is the right kind of explanation, or that temporal asymmetry is a fundamental physical fact at all.

- **Price’s constructivism / temporal bias.** Huw Price argues that many physical theories **presuppose** the very temporal directionality they claim to explain. He proposes that our sense of a one-way arrow is a **cognitive and linguistic construct**—“temporal bias”—rather than an objective feature of reality. From a “view from outside” time, the laws are symmetric, and the asymmetry is an artifact of our perspective.

- **Humean denial / empiricist rejection.** Some philosophers argue that the Past Hypothesis is **otiose** under a Humean best-system account of laws: it need not be added as a fundamental postulate because the best systematization of regularities does not require it.

- **Layer-phenomenological model.** A more integrative alternative proposes that time has **multiple layers**: a physical layer (entropy, initial conditions), a phenomenal layer (conscious experience of flow), and an existential-conceptual layer. The Past Hypothesis is situated only in the physical layer, and the model aims to avoid reductionism by treating temporal directionality as a **multi-dimensional phenomenon**.

### 🌌 Anthropic and Multiverse Alternatives

These approaches explain the low-entropy past by **selection effects** rather than by a special law or boundary condition.

- **Multiverse plus selection.** If there is a vast multiverse with varying initial conditions, observers will necessarily find themselves in those regions that are hospitable to life—and low-entropy beginnings may be a prerequisite for complex observers. The Past Hypothesis is then replaced by an **anthropic selection effect** operating over a multiverse.

- **Cosmological natural selection.** Lee Smolin’s proposal suggests that universes may “reproduce” through black holes, with slight variations in parameters, leading to a kind of **natural selection** that could favor low-entropy initial conditions.

### ♻️ Cyclic and Bounce Alternatives

These models attempt to explain the low-entropy past as a **recurrent** feature of cosmic cycles.

- **Conformal cyclic cosmology (Penrose).** In Penrose’s CCC, the remote future of one “aeon” becomes the big bang of the next. Black holes evaporate, leaving a universe of pure energy and **vanishing entropy**, which then seeds a new low-entropy beginning.

- **Entropy reset mechanisms.** Some cyclic models propose specific mechanisms—such as **geometric mode depletion** or **superluminal expansion**—that reset entropy at the bounce, yielding a low-entropy initial condition for each new cycle without fine-tuning.

“Causality is derivative and not fundamental” is a metaphysical thesis about **priority**, not about whether causation is real or useful. It means:

> Causal facts—facts like “c caused e”—are not part of the basic furniture of reality. They obtain **in virtue of**, are **grounded in**, **supervene on**, or are **reducible to** more fundamental, non-causal facts.

So causation can still be real, objective, and indispensable for explanation, while not being *fundamental*. Compare: “temperature” is real, but it is derivative of molecular motion; “liquidity” is real, but it is derivative of molecular arrangement. On this view, causation is like that—a higher-level feature of the world.

### What “fundamental” means here

A feature is **fundamental** if it is not analyzable in terms of anything more basic, not grounded in anything else, and belongs in the ultimate ontological inventory—whatever that turns out to be: fields, particles, spacetime, quantum states, causal relations, powers, etc.

A feature is **derivative** if it exists “because” more basic facts obtain. For causation, that could mean:

- **Humean regularity:** “c causes e” reduces to c-type events being constantly conjoined with e-type events, plus temporal priority.
- **Counterfactual:** “c causes e” means if c had not occurred, e would not have occurred—grounded in laws and initial conditions.
- **Interventionist:** causal relations are stable patterns under interventions.
- **Mechanistic/conserved-quantity:** causation is the transfer or conservation of physical quantities.
- **Eliminativist/anti-realist:** “cause” is a useful folk concept with no deep ontological counterpart.

In all these, causation is not a primitive relation in the world. It is a higher-level description of non-causal facts.

### The opposing view

The denial is **causal primitivism** or a **powers ontology**:

- Causal relations are irreducible and fundamental.
- Objects have intrinsic causal powers or dispositions; laws of nature derive from those powers.
- In **causal set theory**, the fundamental structure is a causal partial order, and spacetime emerges from it.

On these views, causation is not derivative—it is part of the base level.

### Is it true?

There is no consensus. It is a live metaphysical debate, not a settled empirical fact. The answer depends on what you mean by “causation,” what you count as “fundamental,” and which metaphysical framework you adopt.

**Reasons to think it is true (causation is derivative):**

- Fundamental physics does not seem to contain causation. Newton’s equations, Maxwell’s equations, Schrödinger’s equation, and general relativity describe how states evolve or correlate; they do not include “cause” as a variable.
- The laws of fundamental physics are largely time-reversal symmetric. The causal arrow seems to come from thermodynamics, initial conditions, or our perspective—not from the basic equations.
- Causation is multiply realizable: the same causal pattern can be implemented in neurons, silicon, or hydraulic systems. That suggests it is a higher-level pattern, not a fundamental relation.
- Hume’s point: we never observe a causal “glue” or necessary connection; we observe regularities and sequences.
- Many successful philosophical accounts analyze causation in non-causal terms.

**Reasons to think it is false (causation is fundamental):**

- Causation seems indispensable to explanation, manipulation, agency, and contrastive “why” questions. If it were merely derivative, it is unclear why it tracks objective asymmetries so well.
- Interventionist accounts often seem to presuppose causal notions—what counts as an “intervention” is itself a causal concept—so the reduction may be circular.
- Dispositional essentialists argue that powers are basic and laws are derivative from them.
- Causal set theory and some quantum-gravity approaches treat causal order as primitive.
- Some philosophers argue that experimental science itself presupposes causal relations, so causation cannot be entirely eliminated from the fundamental picture.

### Bottom line

“Causality is derivative and not fundamental” means causation is a higher-level, grounded feature of reality rather than a basic one. Whether that is true is contested. In philosophy of physics, the view that causation is not fundamental is common, even standard in some circles. In general metaphysics, it is much more disputed. So the claim is not simply true or false; it is a metaphysical stance. It is plausible under a Humean or reductionist framework, and false under a causal-primitivist or powers-based ontology.

This is also why the causal theory of time is bold: it needs causation to be fundamental. If causation is derivative, then the causal arrow cannot ground time’s arrow—it is just one more mini-arrow downstream of something else.

**Bell’s theorem** says something like this: if two entangled particles have pre-set properties (“hidden variables”) and there is no faster-than-light influence between them, then the correlations you see when you measure them must obey certain limits—called Bell inequalities. Experiments violate those limits. So you seem forced to give up at least one of these:

1. **Realism** — particles have definite properties before measurement.  
2. **Locality** — no influence travels faster than light.  
3. **Statistical independence** — the measurement settings are not secretly correlated with the particles’ hidden properties.

**Superdeterminism** is a way to keep locality and hidden variables by denying assumption 3.

It says: the experimenter’s choice of what to measure—say, which direction to set a detector—is not truly free or independent of the particles. Instead, everything, including the experimenter’s decision, the random-number generator, the detector settings, and the particles’ hidden states, was determined from the Big Bang onward and is all correlated.

So when Alice and Bob measure entangled particles and get weirdly strong correlations, it’s not because the particles communicate faster than light. It’s because the whole experiment was “rigged” from the start: the detector settings and the particle properties were secretly coordinated by initial conditions.

An analogy: imagine two students taking a test and giving identical unusual answers. If they studied independently, that’s suspicious. But if the teacher wrote both answer keys and secretly coordinated everything, their matching answers don’t prove they cheated by telepathy. Superdeterminism says the universe is like that teacher: the particles and the measurement choices share a common causal past.

Why is it called a **loophole**? Because Bell’s theorem only rules out local hidden variables *if* the measurement settings are statistically independent of the hidden variables. Superdeterminism says they are not independent. So the theorem’s conclusion doesn’t apply.

Why is it controversial? Because it requires a huge, conspiracy-like correlation between the experimenter’s choices and the particles. It can seem to undermine the whole point of doing experiments, since it says your choice of measurement was never independent of what you’re measuring. Many physicists regard it as an implausible last resort, though a few take it seriously.

So in one sentence: **Superdeterminism is the idea that the universe is so thoroughly deterministic and pre-correlated that Bell test experiments are not really independent tests, allowing local hidden variables to survive.**
